## How to Control Lawyers

### What You Need to Know

#### Self-Interest

Lawyers are out for themselves.

No exception.

That is what self-interest means.

Whether they win or lose your case, most of them still go home, sleep in the same bed, wake up the next morning, and keep moving.

You do not.

You are the one who lives with the outcome.

You are the one who may be left with prison time, impossible debt, court costs, family damage, or the wreckage of a judgment you cannot afford.

That is the first thing you need to get straight.

Everybody operates from self-interest.

You do.
I do.
Everybody does.

People want what they want, and they move toward it.

Sometimes that is money.
Sometimes it is praise.
Sometimes it is comfort, safety, love, ego, status, control, or relief.

But it is still self-interest.

Lawyers are no different.

And that matters because their self-interest affects your life.

It may be your lawyer.
It may be the lawyer on the other side.

Either way, if you do not understand what drives them, you are exposed.

This section is about seeing the legal world through their eyes, understanding what motivates them, and learning enough to protect yourself from getting played.

#### What Lawyers Tell Clients

Most lawyers know how to make an impression.

They are cordial.
Polite.
Friendly.
Smooth when they need to be.

They know how to shake a hand, ask about your family, remember a dog's name, and make you feel like you just found somebody solid and safe in the middle of a mess.

Do not let that fool you.

The time to decide whether a lawyer is really your friend is after the case is over, the money has stopped flowing, and the lawyer still has a reason to show up with nothing to gain.

That does happen sometimes.

Not often enough to build your judgment around it.

When you hire a lawyer, deal with the relationship for what it is:

you are hiring someone to do a job.

Not looking for emotional comfort.
Not shopping for a new best friend.
Not paying for charm.

You are paying for performance.

And if the job is not done well, the friendliness means nothing.

If a lawyer tells you, `We're going to win this case,` do not just melt into relief and nod.

Ask questions.

Ask:

- What exactly are you going to do to win?
- What is your plan?
- Is your plan written down?
- When do I get to see it?

If a lawyer says, `Don't worry, I'll handle everything,` treat that like a warning flare.

You do not want somebody `handling everything` while you sit blind.

You want to be deeply involved.

It is your case.
Your risk.
Your life.

You should know what the lawyer plans to do before it is done whenever possible, and you should understand why.

Lawyers who want you passive usually want your money active.

Do not go for that.

If a lawyer says, `You won't pay anything unless I win,` get it in writing.

Not in a vague reassuring tone.
Not in a handshake.
Not in some loose verbal promise.

In writing.

Make sure the agreement is clear about fees, costs, depositions, travel, experts, postage, everything.

Take the contract home.
Read it slowly.
Read it again.
Then decide.

Also remember this:

the lawyer's percentage is negotiable.

Just because someone asks for one-third does not mean one-third is the only number available.

If your case is strong and the upside is real, negotiate.

And make sure the contract says the lawyer needs your written consent before agreeing to any settlement, stipulation, or move that limits your recovery.

Do not leave that open.

Too many people find out too late that their lawyer made a deal without real permission and then acted like it was normal.

Whatever a lawyer promises you before you sign, get it in writing.

If the lawyer will not put it in writing, keep looking.

There are more lawyers.
Plenty more.

Be selective.

Choose with your eyes open.

#### What Lawyers Do Not Tell Clients

Your lawyer is not going to lead with the parts that make you hesitate.

They are not going to volunteer that they struggled through the bar exam.

They are not going to casually admit they do not really know how to use core discovery tools properly.

They are not going to tell you they are confused about the law in your case and plan to quietly crowdsource their way through it.

They are not going to tell you they are close friends with opposing counsel.

They are not going to tell you the judge assigned to your case already dislikes them or has sanctioned them before.

And a lot of them are definitely not going to tell you when they drag a case out because more time means more billing.

Worse, some will not tell you when the case is unwinnable because admitting that stops the money.

That is real.

You need to understand that before you hand somebody control.

#### How Lawyers Bill Time

If the lawyer is not working on contingency, the billing starts fast and adds up faster.

Many bill in tenths of an hour.

That means a short phone call costs money.

A quick email costs money.

Some bill absurd minimum chunks no matter how small the task is.

Some bill for `thinking`.

That should tell you everything you need to know about why you must stay alert.

Lawyers sell two things:

- knowledge
- time

And time is where they make a lot of their money.

That is exactly why you need to know what they are doing, why it matters, and what comes next before you pay the bill.

A lot of what lawyers do is not necessary to win.

Require a time sheet.

Not a vague monthly total.

A time sheet.

Make them show what was done, how long it took, and how it moved your case forward.

If they billed half an hour for a call with opposing counsel, ask what was discussed and why it mattered.

If you are paying for it, you have the right to know.

#### Why Lawyers Take Depositions Too Soon

Some lawyers love expensive activity because expensive activity looks like progress to clients who do not know better.

Depositions are a classic example.

There are lawyers who will schedule depositions early and often when much of what they need could have been obtained first in writing for a fraction of the cost.

That is not strategy.
That is often billing.

Written discovery should usually do a lot of the early heavy lifting.

Then, if loose ends remain, depositions may make sense.

If you are paying by the hour, do not tolerate unnecessary deposition theater.

And if your lawyer does take depositions, be present and pay attention.

Do not let the questioning drift into useless wandering.

Depositions are for building the record, not socializing with opposing counsel.

#### A Lawyer's Typical Work Day

A lawyer worth paying should be spending time on:

- legal research
- drafting
- hearings
- preparation

Everything else should be questioned hard.

#### Professional Responsibility Standards

Every state bar has rules for lawyer conduct.

They may call them professional responsibility standards, ethics rules, or something similar, but they exist and they matter.

Read them.

Know them.

Use them.

They are one of the few tools ordinary people have to push back when a lawyer starts sliding into conduct that serves the lawyer more than the client or the system.

#### What Non-Lawyers Can Do

If you are not a lawyer, there are things you may be able to do that a lawyer cannot.

For example, lawyers usually cannot talk directly to represented opposing parties.

You may be able to, depending on your jurisdiction and the posture of the case.

That does not mean be reckless.

It does mean you should understand that being a non-lawyer can create certain tactical freedoms that lawyers do not have.

Check the rules first.

Then move carefully.

There are advantages to not being a lawyer.

### Conclusion

If you understand how lawyers think, you will stop being surprised by them.

That alone gives you leverage.

Do not assume they think like you.

Do not assume your interests come first for them.

Sometimes you will find a good one.

Sometimes you will find somebody polished, expensive, and completely wrong for you.

You need to know the difference before it costs you everything.

## How to Control Judges

### Insight Based on Experience

The first rule with judges is simple:

they are human.

That matters more than people want it to.

Sometimes you may look at the judge in your case and think you are dealing with a gorilla in a black robe.

Sometimes maybe you are.

But most are not.

Most are human beings carrying the same weight everybody else carries:

family pressure, health worries, fatigue, deadlines, distractions, bad moods, private fear, ego, conscience, and the possibility that by the time your case is called they are already mentally drained.

That does not excuse bad judging.

It does explain why pretending judges are pure machines of law is naive.

Some judges may care more about getting your case right than you realize.

Some may have already formed impressions before the hearing even starts based on your credibility, your opponent's reputation, prior conduct in the file, or what they know about the lawyers in the room.

That happens.

Judges are not identical.

No two are.

Most are trying to do the right thing as best they can.

None are perfect.

So stop expecting perfection.

That is fantasy.

The better move is to prepare for the worst while being wise enough to understand that judges respond to tone, respect, order, and human signals just like everyone else.

Kindness works.

Politeness works.

Respect works.

Rudeness usually backfires.

If you want a judge to rule in your favor, do not poison the room with contempt and then act shocked when the bench hardens against you.

The Golden Rule applies here too.

Treat the judge the way you would want to be treated if you were the one holding the power to decide.

That will not guarantee a good result.

It does maximize the chance that you will not sabotage yourself before the legal argument even gets heard.

Your case can be won or lost on how you handle the judge.

That is not an exaggeration.

Judges rule.

Getting them to rule in your favor is the objective.

### Get the Judge's Attention

Do not assume the judge is locked in on you just because you are speaking.

Some judges drift.

Some read.
Some type.
Some stare off into nowhere.
Some are tired enough to slide toward sleep.

If that happens, get their attention back.

Respectfully.

You can ask:

- `Did I make that last point clear, Your Honor?`
- `Would it help if I spoke a little louder, Your Honor?`

The point is not to embarrass the judge.

The point is to be heard.

If the judge is not listening, you may technically be making a record, but you are still risking a bad ruling you will have to spend time and money fighting later.

Make eye contact.
Hold it.

At the start of a hearing, ask whether the judge read your motion.

If the answer is yes, good.

If the answer is no, do not just nod and keep moving like nothing happened.

Read the critical parts out loud.

Make sure the judge hears what you are asking for and why the law supports it.

If you do not do that, you are giving away ground for free.

When you are speaking and the judge looks away, pause.

Wait.

And if necessary, ask politely for the Court's attention.

This is your case.

You are not there to mumble into the air while power looks somewhere else.

Also, do not get sloppy with titles.

Call the judge `Your Honor`.
Refer to the court as `the Court`.

That may feel small.

Small things change tone.
Tone changes outcomes.

Respect matters.

### Objections and Judicial Error

When something improper starts happening, move fast.

Stand up.

Say `Objection.`

If you know the basis, say it.

And say it clearly enough that the error stops before it sinks into the record any deeper than it already has.

But force does not mean disrespect.

The job is to be firm without becoming wild, angry, childish, or theatrical.

If the judge overrules you and the error matters, object again if necessary.

Then move if you need to move.

Then file if you need to file.

Because if the error stands and the record is muddy, appeal gets harder and justice gets farther away.

And yes, in extreme moments, if judicial behavior itself becomes part of the problem, you may need to make that clear on the record too.

But use that with discipline.

Not ego.

Not frustration.

Only when the record truly needs it.

### Getting Orders

The whole point of going to court is to get orders signed.

That is the game.

Motions are filed to get orders.
Hearings are argued to get orders.
Cases are won by getting orders.

A major problem comes when a hearing ends and the judge has not clearly ruled.

Do not just gather your papers and leave like that is normal.

Ask for the ruling.

If it is your motion, ask for the ruling on your motion.
If it is the other side's motion, ask for the ruling on that.

Make the judge decide.

And make the decision clear.

If the judge refuses to rule, object to the refusal and make sure the record captures that.

Then, when a ruling is made, ask if you may prepare the order.

Do that fast.

Because if your opponent drafts the order first, there is a real chance the written version will say what helps them instead of what the judge actually said.

If you are allowed to draft it, do it carefully.

Use the judge's actual words where you can.

If necessary, get the transcript and draft from that.

Then send the proposed order the proper way, with copies to the other side and the right supporting paperwork.

This is not some minor clerical step.

This is one of the places cases get stolen in plain sight by people who know the other side is not paying attention.

Do not trust your opponent to write your victory down accurately.

Ever.

### Stop Chasing Fake Internet Tricks

Do not waste time with fantasy arguments about seeing the judge's oath, fringe on the flag, all-caps names, or any other internet-fed nonsense people use when they do not know how court actually works.

Those tactics do not save real cases.

What they do is mark you as unserious, anti-system, and easier to dismiss.

If you want to win, use pleadings, motions, discovery, evidence, objections, and record-making the right way.

That is how court power is navigated.

Not by silver-bullet folklore.

### Make the Record

If you do not make a record, you lose control.

That is not dramatic. That is just true.

If no record exists, the judge can say almost anything later and you will have very little to fight with.

Never appear before a judge unless a record is being made.

Court reporter.
Official recording.
Something you can actually use later.

If the ruling goes wrong and there is no record, appeal becomes a ghost hunt.

You have a right to be heard.

You also have a right to make a record.

Use both.

### Conclusion

Judges are human beings with enormous power.

Some are thoughtful.
Some are careless.
Some are fair.
Some should never have been given the robe.

That is reality.

So handle judges with care.

Be respectful.
Be sharp.
Be organized.
Be impossible to ignore.
Make the record.
Get the ruling.
Get the order.

That is how you make judicial power work for you instead of against you.

## How to Collect Judgments

### Getting What You Fought For

Most lawsuits are about money.

Not symbolism.
Not abstract vindication.
Not a framed court order you can stare at while your bills keep coming.

Money.

That is why judgment collection matters.

Because the judge usually does not order somebody to walk over and hand you cash.

The judge signs an order saying you won and the other side owes you.

That order is still just paper unless it contains the language that gives it teeth.

The phrase that matters is:

`... for which let execution issue.`

Without that, a judgment can be almost worthless in practical terms.

With it, the clerk can issue a writ of execution.

And that is where court power turns into collection power.

### Why That Phrase Matters

Your electric bill says you owe money.

Credit card statements say you owe money.

Neither of those says `for which let execution issue`.

That is the difference.

A judgment that only says the debtor owes you something has very limited force.

A judgment that authorizes execution opens the door to actual seizure of non-exempt property.

That matters because you did not go through all this just to collect a piece of paper.

You went through it to collect value.

### What a Writ of Execution Does

A writ of execution is the order that empowers the sheriff to seize non-exempt property of the judgment debtor and sell it until the judgment is paid, along with the sheriff's lawful costs.

Without the writ, the sheriff has no authority to touch the debtor's property.

Without the writ, you can win and still collect nothing.

That is why the writ is not some technical afterthought.

It is one of the main reasons the judgment matters.

### Using a Writ of Execution

If you want to collect through execution, here is the basic path:

1. Make sure the final judgment includes the language `for which let execution issue`.
2. Use post-judgment discovery to find the debtor's non-exempt property.
3. Get the writ from the clerk.
4. Get the writ to the sheriff.
5. Tell the sheriff what property exists and where it is.
6. Pay any required advance fees or bond.
7. Wait while the sheriff seizes, advertises, and sells the property.

That is the mechanics.

If you are not prepared to pursue collection after winning, then you need to ask yourself why you filed the lawsuit in the first place.

There is almost nothing more exhausting than fighting all the way to judgment and ending up with nothing usable.

### Garnishment

If the debtor does not have collectible non-exempt property, garnishment may be the next path.

That can allow levy on non-exempt earnings or other reachable funds, depending on your jurisdiction.

But garnishment is usually slower and weaker than direct levy on property.

It matters.

It can work.

It is just not usually the fastest route to money.

And always verify what income is exempt under current law.

Exemptions change.

### Turning Paper Into Cash

A court order does not buy groceries.

It does not pay your utility bill.

It does not replace the time you lost getting it.

So if you want the judgment to mean something, you need to think like a collector before you even sue.

Ask yourself:

1. Does the defendant own any non-exempt property, and what is it worth?
2. Does the defendant have cash, bank funds, or liquid assets?
3. Does the defendant have stocks, bonds, insurance cash value, or other investment assets?
4. Is bankruptcy likely?

If the defendant has nothing you can realistically collect, your lawsuit may be a waste even if you win.

That is a hard truth, but it is still true.

You cannot get blood from a turnip.

And suing an uncollectible defendant may leave you with a judgment and no money to show for the effort.

On the other hand, if the defendant owns real value and it is reachable, then now you know what you are really fighting for.

### Get Smart Before You Sue

Before filing, find out what you can about the defendant's assets.

Search public records.
Check for prior judgments.
Look at liens.
Notice the lifestyle, but do not be fooled by appearances.

Some people look broke and have assets.
Some people look rich and are drowning in debt.

If needed, hire an investigator before spending much more on litigation.

A few dollars upfront can save you thousands later.

### The Clerk and the Sheriff

Once you have a proper judgment, the clerk can issue the writ if the judgment authorizes it.

If you are pro se, go to the clerk's office in person.

Bring the case number.
Bring your judgment information.
Ask what needs to be done.

Follow instructions exactly.

The sheriff's civil division usually handles the actual levy process.

Once the writ is docketed and the sheriff knows what property to look for, the process moves into the enforcement stage.

You may have to post funds or a bond to cover costs.

If enough property is seized and sold, those costs are usually covered out of the process.

### Sample Execution Language

The basic writ is simple in concept.

It tells the sheriff to levy on the debtor's property, satisfy the amount due, and return the writ when satisfied.

What matters is not elegance.

What matters is authority.

The writ transfers enforceable power into the hands of the officer who can actually act on it.

### Interest Matters

Not all judgments carry the same interest consequences.

There is:

- post-judgment interest
- pre-judgment interest

Post-judgment interest usually starts when the final judgment is entered.

Pre-judgment interest is based on a point before judgment, often tied to the date of loss, default, or other legally recognized date.

If you do not make sure pre-judgment interest is included where it belongs, you can lose a lot of money.

That is not small.

That is real recovery walking out the door.

#### Pre-Judgment Interest

`Promissory Notes`

Defaulted interest-bearing notes should generally include both pre-judgment and post-judgment interest in the final judgment.

And good note drafting matters here.

Acceleration clauses matter.
Higher lawful default-interest clauses matter.
Attorney-fee clauses matter.

If those terms are missing, you may end up recovering far less than you should.

`Contracts`

Some contracts also provide for interest on unpaid balances.

If the contract is drafted properly and the claim asks for it, the final judgment should include the interest.

And that leads to a critical warning:

If you do not ask for interest in the complaint, the court may deny it.

If you do not ask for attorney's fees where the contract allows them, the court may deny those too.

Do not assume the judge will fix your pleading mistakes for you.

### Post-Judgment Discovery

Before you can collect, you need to know what the debtor owns.

That means post-judgment discovery.

Possible tools may include:

- requests for admissions
- requests for production
- interrogatories
- depositions
- subpoenas and related court orders

One of the strongest tools is often the deposition in aid of execution.

That is where you pin the debtor down under oath about:

- real estate
- vehicles
- personal property
- collectibles
- intellectual property
- insurance
- trusts and inheritances
- bank accounts
- debts owed to the debtor
- employment
- wages
- other income
- pending claims
- liabilities
- monthly expenses

The point is not curiosity.

The point is finding collectible assets.

### Levy and Property Types

A levy is the actual seizure.

That is the state using lawful force to take possession of property despite the debtor's resistance.

Three general kinds of property may be subject to execution:

- real property
- tangible personal property
- intangible property

Some property is exempt.

Homestead may be exempt in some states.
Part of wages may be exempt.
Other protected property may be exempt by state or federal law.

Know the exemptions before you act.

### Renewing Judgments

If you cannot collect now, do not assume the judgment is dead forever.

People's circumstances change.

A broke debtor today may have assets later.

But judgments can expire.

That means renewal matters.

The process differs by state, and the timelines matter a lot.

Miss the renewal window and you may lose the judgment's power.

So if the debtor has nothing today, track the expiration date anyway.

A lot can change over a few years.

### Satisfaction of Judgment

Once the judgment is paid in full, a satisfaction of judgment should be filed.

That formally shows the debt is satisfied.

This matters for credit, records, and finality.

And if you are the debtor paying directly, protect yourself.

Get signed, dated receipts.
Get proof of everything.
Get descriptions of any property taken.

Because a dishonest creditor can take payment and still pretend otherwise if you leave yourself with no evidence.

### Conclusion

An uncollectible judgment is close to worthless.

That is why collection strategy matters almost as much as winning.

Know whether the defendant is collectible before you sue.

Know how to use execution.
Know how to use discovery.
Know how to find assets.
Know how to preserve interest.
Know how to renew.

If you cannot collect at the end, the victory is mostly theater.

If you can collect, then the judgment finally becomes what you fought for in the first place.

## Forms

### Winning Your Case on Paper

Form without content is decoration.

It is a picture frame with nothing inside it.

Content is what matters.

Content wins.
Content persuades.
Content survives scrutiny.

Formatting rules may matter locally, but formatting does not rescue empty substance.

That is the first principle.

You do not need forms because forms are magical.

You need to understand what each form is supposed to do.

That means knowing:

- what task the form performs
- what goal the form must achieve
- what must be said
- what cannot be omitted

Everything else is secondary.

Margins.
Fonts.
Paper size.
Spacing.
Vertical lines.

Those things can matter for compliance.

They do not carry your case.

### Content First

This section is not about mindlessly copying templates.

It is about understanding how forms work so you can draft what you actually need instead of blindly filling blanks in something you do not understand.

Winning lawsuits is not baking from a recipe card.

There is no universal plug-in form that guarantees a favorable judgment.

There is only structure, purpose, and content.

Local formatting rules can be adjusted.

The core function of the document stays the same.

So learn the sequence.
Learn what comes first.
Learn what comes next.
Learn what each part is there to accomplish.

That is where the value is.

### Core Forms

You only need a relatively small group of forms to handle most lawsuits well:

- complaint
- motions directed to the complaint
- motion to dismiss
- motion to strike
- motion for more definite statement
- answer
- affirmative defenses
- reply to affirmative defenses
- discovery forms
- requests for admissions
- requests for production
- interrogatories
- deposition scheduling forms
- subpoenas
- motions to compel discovery
- motion to compel production
- motion to compel better answers to interrogatories
- motion to deem admissions admitted
- motion to compel attendance
- motion to show cause
- motion for contempt
- memoranda
- notices
- trial forms
- notice for trial
- pre-trial stipulation
- motion in limine
- jury instructions
- orders

Most other forms are just variations built from the same logic.

So know what is required.

Put in what serves a real purpose.

Leave out what does not.

Then stop writing.

Because one of the most common mistakes in legal drafting is saying too much.

The next most common mistake is failing to edit.

Edit hard.

Print it.
Read it on paper.
Read it out loud.
See if it makes sense to another person.

If it does not make sense to a friend, it probably will not make sense to a judge either.

Editing pays off.

### Summons

The summons is not a casual notice.

It is a command from the court.

It is also a warning.

Once the court is satisfied that the summons was properly served, the court treats the defendant as subject to its personal jurisdiction.

And if the defendant does not respond, default can follow.

That is why the summons matters.

It is the document that pulls the defendant under the court's power in a practical way.

In many jurisdictions the clerk prepares and signs the summons.

If you ever do need to prepare one yourself, the structure still matters more than visual polish:

it must clearly communicate the command, the case, the response deadline, and the consequences of ignoring it.
