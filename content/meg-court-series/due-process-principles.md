# Due Process: The Rights That Protect You in Court

Due process is not a technicality. It is the principle that protects every person's right to be heard before the state acts against them.

## The Core Rule

A judge who decides without hearing both sides acts unjustly — even if the outcome happens to be technically correct. Process is not separate from justice. It is part of how justice is measured.

If a court:
- denied you notice of a hearing
- held a hearing without allowing you to present witnesses
- relied on reports or evidence you were not given a chance to challenge
- made a ruling without sworn testimony or a factual record

...those are due process violations. Document them. Raise them. They are the basis for appeals and civil rights claims.

## The Burden of Proof Stays on the Party Making the Claim

The party demanding action, money, custody change, or relief carries the burden of proving why the court should act. This is not automatic — you have to make them carry it.

If the other side is asking the court to restrict your parenting time, remove your children, or hold you in contempt, they must prove the facts that justify that relief. If they have not proved it, say so. Make the record reflect the gap between what they claimed and what they proved.

In custody cases, this matters constantly. Allegations alone are not evidence. Reports from GALs or agencies are not automatically reliable. Ask: what is the actual admissible evidence behind this claim?

## Rights During Court-Appointed Processes

If a Guardian ad Litem, parenting evaluator, or therapist has been appointed in your case:

- You have the right to review their reports before relying on them in court
- You have the right to cross-examine them if they testify
- You can object to hearsay and unverified statements in their reports
- You can challenge their methodology and qualifications
- You can request the underlying records they relied on

These professionals are subject to examination just like any witness. Their appointment does not make their findings immune from challenge.

## What to Do When Process Is Violated

If you believe your due process rights were violated:

1. **Make a record at the time** — object on the record, state the basis. "Your Honor, I was not given notice of this hearing" or "I object to the court relying on this report without an opportunity to cross-examine." If you do not raise it, you may waive it.

2. **File a motion** — a motion for reconsideration, a motion to set aside the order, or a motion for a new hearing. Attach your documented basis.

3. **Appeal** — due process violations are grounds for appeal. The standard of review depends on the type of violation, but courts of appeal take them seriously.

4. **Civil rights** — if state actors (judges, agencies, court-appointed professionals) violated your constitutional rights under color of law, 42 U.S.C. § 1983 provides a federal cause of action. This is a serious step that requires specific elements, but it exists.

## The Practical Standard

Courts exist so ordinary people have a place where they can compel power to obey law. That includes compelling the court itself to follow the rules. You have standing to make that demand. Use it.
