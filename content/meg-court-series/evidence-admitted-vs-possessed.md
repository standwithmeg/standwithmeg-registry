# Evidence: What Gets In and What Stays Out

Something is not evidence just because you have it in your hand. Until it is admitted into the court record, it is just material. This distinction costs people cases.

Admissibility is what turns raw material into legal force. And blocking the other side's bad material matters just as much as getting your own in.

## The Six Evidence Filters

Every piece of offered evidence has to survive these six filters:

**Relevance** — Does this fact tend to prove or disprove something that actually matters in this case? Irrelevant material does not belong in the record, no matter how dramatic it is. Emotional side stories, character attacks with no bearing on the legal issues, and facts that do not move any disputed element should be objected to as irrelevant.

**Reliability** — Is the source stable enough to trust? Impaired witnesses, unsupported hearsay chains, and unverifiable material fail this filter. Courts are not supposed to rely on evidence that cannot be grounded in something dependable.

**Competence** — Does the witness have first-hand knowledge of what they are testifying to? A witness cannot testify about what another person said and treat it as fact — that is hearsay. Important practical point: lawyers are not witnesses. If opposing counsel starts telling the court what happened as if argument is testimony, shut that down. Facts must come from admissible sources.

**Credibility** — Is this source believable? Internally inconsistent testimony, known dishonesty, or implausible claims all go to credibility. This filter matters even when formal exclusion is not available — making clear that something should not be believed is still evidence work.

**Prejudice** — Even relevant evidence can be excluded if its prejudicial effect substantially outweighs its probative value. Courts can keep out material that is designed to inflame rather than inform.

**Privilege** — Some communications are protected: attorney-client, doctor-patient, spousal privilege in some contexts. Privileged information stays out even if it is relevant.

## Objections Are How You Enforce This in Real Time

An objection without legal grounds is noise. An objection with legal grounds stops everything until the judge rules.

When you object, name the filter: "Objection — hearsay." "Objection — relevance." "Objection — lacks foundation." The judge cannot rule on a vague "I object." Be specific.

If you do not object, improper material can be admitted and relied on. You cannot object after the fact to evidence you let in without challenge.

## Common Hearsay Traps in Family Court

GAL reports, therapist summaries, and agency reports often contain hearsay — statements from third parties, children, or others repeated without the original speaker being available for cross-examination. These documents can be powerful against you if admitted without challenge.

When you see hearsay in a report:
- Object to the specific hearsay statements, not the whole document
- Request that the original source be made available for examination
- Ask the court to identify which hearsay exception, if any, applies

Hearsay exceptions exist (excited utterance, present sense impression, business records) — knowing them helps you challenge weak claims to those exceptions.
